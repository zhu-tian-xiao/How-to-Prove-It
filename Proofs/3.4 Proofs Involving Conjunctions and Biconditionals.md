The method for proving a goal of the form $P\land Q$ is very simple:

To prove a goal of the form $P\land Q$:

Prove $P$ and $Q$ separately.

In other words, a goal of the form $P\land Q$ is treated as two separate goals: $P$, and $Q$. The same is true of givens of the form $P\land Q$:

To use a given of the form $P\land Q$:
Treat this given as two separate givens: $P$, and $Q$.

Example 3.4.1. Suppose $A\subseteq B$, and $A$ and $C$ are disjoint. Prove that $A\subseteq B\setminus C$.

Scratch work

| Givens              | Goal                      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------- |
| $A\subseteq B$      | $A\subseteq B\setminus C$ |
| $A\cap C=\emptyset$ |                           |

| Givens              | Goals        |
| ------------------- | ------------ |
| $A\subseteq B$      | $x \in B$    |
| $A\cap C=\emptyset$ | $x\not\in C$ |
| $x \in A$           |              |

The final proof will have this form:

$$
\begin{align}
&\text{Let }x\text{ be arbitrary.} \\
&\quad\text{Suppose }x \in A. \\
&\quad\quad\text{[Proof of }x \in B\text{ goes here.]} \\
&\quad\quad\text{[Proof of }x \not\in C\text{ goes here.]} \\ 
&\quad\quad\text{Thus, }x \in B\land x\not\in C\text{, so }x \in B\setminus C. \\
&\quad\text{Therefore }x \in A\to x \in B\setminus C. \\
&\text{Since }x\text{ was arbitrary, }\forall x(x \in A\to x \in B\setminus C)\text{, so}A\subseteq B\setminus C.
\end{align}
$$

Theorem. Suppose $A\subseteq B$, and $A$ an $A$ and $C$ are disjoint. Then $A\subseteq B\setminus C$. 

Proof. Suppose $x \in A$. Since $A\subseteq B$, it follows that $x \in B$, and since $A$ and $C$ are disjoint, we must have $x\not\in C$. Thus, $x \in B\setminus C$. Since $x$ was an arbitrary element of $A$, we can conclude that $A\subseteq B\setminus C$.

Because $P\leftrightarrow Q$ is equivalent to $(P\to Q)\land(Q\to P)$, according to our strategies a given of the form $P\leftrightarrow Q$ should be treated as two separate givens or goals: $P\to Q$, and $Q\to P$.

To prove a goal of the form $P\leftrightarrow Q$:
Prove $P\to Q$ and $Q\to P$ separately.

To use a given of the form $P\leftrightarrow Q$:
Treat this as two separate givens: $P\to Q$, and $Q\to P$.

Example 3.4.3. Suppose $x$ is an integer. Prove that $x$ is even iff $x^2$ is even.

Theorem. Suppose $x$ is an integer. Then $x$ is even iff $x^2$ is even.

Proof. ($\to$) Suppose $x$ is even. Then for some integer $k$, $x=2k$. Therefore, $x^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)$, so since $2k^2$ is an integer, $x^2$ is even. Thus, if $x$ is even then $x^2$ is even.

($\leftarrow$) Suppose $x$ is odd. Then $x=2k+1$ for some integer $k$. Therefore, $x^2=(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1$, so since $2k^2+2k$ is an integer, $x^2$ is odd. Thus, if $x^2$ is even then $x$ is even.

Example 3.4..4. Prove that $\forall x\neg P(x)\leftrightarrow \neg \exists xP(x)$.

Scratch work
($\to$) We must prove $\forall x\neg P(x)\to \neg \exists xP(x)$, so we assume $\forall x\neg P(x)$ and try to prove $\neg \exists xP(x)$. Our goal is now a negated statement, and reexpressing it would require the use of the very equivalence that we are trying to prove! We therefore fall back on our only other strategy for dealing with negative goals, proof by contradiction. We now have the following situation:


| Givens               | Goal          |
| -------------------- | ------------- |
| $\forall x\neg P(x)$ | Contradiction |
| $\exists xP(x)$      |               |

The second given starts with an existential quantifier, so we use it immdiately and let $x_{0}$ stand for some object which the statement $P(x_{0})$ is true. But now plugging in $x_{0}$ for $x$ in the first given we can conclude that $\neg P(x_{0})$, which gives us the contradiction we need.

$(\leftarrow)$ For this direction of the biconditional we should assume $\neg\exists xP(x)$ and try to prove $\forall x\neg P(x)$. Because this goal starts with a universal quantifier, we let $x$ be arbitrary and try to prove $\neg P(x)$. Once again, we now have a negated goal that can't be reexpressed, so we use proof by contradiction:

| Givens               | Goal          |
| -------------------- | ------------- |
| $\neg \exists xP(x)$ | Contradiction |
| $P(x)$               |               |

Our first  given is also a negated statement, and this suggests that we could get the contradiction we need by proving $\exists xP(x)$. We therefore set this as our goal.

| Givens               | Goal            |
| -------------------- | --------------- |
| $\neg \exists xP(x)$ | $\exists xP(x)$ |
| $P(x)$               |                 |

To prove this goal we have to find a value of $y$ that makes $P(y)$ come out true. But this is easy! Our second given, $P(x)$, tells us that our arbitrary $x$ is the value we need.

Example 3.4.5. Suppose $A$, $B$, $C$ are sets. Prove that $A\cap (B\setminus C)=(A\cap B)\setminus C$.

Theorem. Suppose $A$, $B$, and $C$ are sets. Then $A\cap(B\setminus C)=(A\cap B)\setminus C$.

Proof. Let $x$ be arbitrary. Then $x \in A\cap(B\setminus C)$ iff $x \in A\land x \in B\setminus C$ iff $x \in A\land x \in B\land x\not\in C$ iff $x \in(A\cap C)\land x\not\in C$ iff $x \in(A\cap B)\setminus C$.

Thus, $\forall x(x \in A\cap(B\setminus C)\leftrightarrow x \in(A\cap B)\setminus C)$, so $A\cap(B\setminus C)=(A\cap B)\setminus C$.
