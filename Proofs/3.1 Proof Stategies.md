# Proof Stategies
Mathematicians usually state the answer to a mathematical question in the form of a **theorem** that says that if certain assumptions called **hypotheses** of the theorem are true, then some conclusion must also be true. Often the hypotheses and conclusionc contain free variables, and in this case it is understood that these variables can stand for any elements of the universe of discourse. An assignment of particular values to these variables is called an **instance** of the theorem, and in order for the theorem that makes the hypotheses come out true, the conclusion is  also true. If there is even one instance in which the hypotheses are true but the conclusion is false, then the theorem is incorrect. Such an instance is called a **counterexample** to the theorem.

Example 3.1.2. Suppose $a$ and $b$ are real numbers. Prove that if $0<a<b$ then $a^2<b^2$.

Theorem. Suppose $a$ and $b$ are real numbers. If $0<a<b$ then $a^2<b^2$.

Proof. Suppose $0<a<b$. Multiplying the inequality $a<b$ by the positive number $a$ we can conclude that $a^2<ab$, and similarly multiplying by $b$ we get $ab<b^2$. Therefore $a^2<ab<b^2$, so $a^2<b^2$, as required. Thus, if $0<a<b$ then $a^2<b^2$.

To prove a conclusion of the form $P\to Q$:
Assume $P$ is true and then prove $Q$.

Form of final proof:

$$
\begin{align}
&\text{Suppose } P. \\
&\quad\text{[Proof of }Q \text{ goes here.]} \\
&\text{Therefore } P\to Q.
\end{align}

$$

To prove a goal of the form $P\to Q$:
Assume $Q$ is false and prove that $P$ is false.

Form of final proof:

$$
\begin{align}
&\text{Suppose } Q\text{ is false}. \\
&\quad\text{[Proof of }\neg P \text{ goes here.]} \\
&\text{Therefore } P\to Q.
\end{align}
$$

Example 3.1.3. Suppose $a$, $b$, and $c$ are real numbers and $a<b$. Prove that if $ac\leq bc$ then $c\leq 0$.

Scratch work


| Given                          | Goal                      |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------- |
| $a$, $b$, $c$ are real numbers | $(ac\leq bc)\to(c\leq 0)$ |
| $a>b$                          |                           |

The contrapositive of the goal is $\neg(c\leq 0)\to \neg(ac\leq bc)$, or in other words $(c>0)\to(ac<bc)$, so we can prove it by adding $c>0$ to the list of givens and making $ac>bc$ our new goal:

| Given                          | Goal    |
| ------------------------------ | ------- |
| $a$, $b$, $c$ are real numbers | $ac>bc$ |
| $a>b$                          |         |
| $c>0$                          |         |

We can also now write the first and last sentences of the proof. According to the strategy, the final proof should have this form:

$$
\begin{align}
&\text{Suppose } c>0. \\
&\quad\text{[Proof of }ac>bc \text{ goes here.]} \\
&\text{Therefore, if } ac\leq bc \text{ then } c\leq 0.
\end{align}
$$

Using the new given $c>0$, we see that the goal $ac>bc$ follows immediately from  the given $a>b$ by multiplying both sides by the positive number $c$. Inserting this step between the first and last sentences completes the proof.

Theorem. Suppose $a$, $b$, and $c$ are real numbers and $a>b$. If $ac\leq bc$ then $c\leq 0$.

Proof. We will prove the contrapositive. Suppose $c>0$. Then we can multiply both sides of the given inequality $a>b$ by $c$ and conclude that $ac>bc$. Therefore, if $ac\leq bc$ then $c\leq 0$.
