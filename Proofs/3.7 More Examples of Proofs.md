Theorem 3.7.1. Suppose $n$ is an integer larger that $1$ and $n$ is not prime. Then $2^n-1$ is not prime.

Proof. Since $n$ is not prime, there are positive integers a and b such that $a<n$, $b<n$, and $n=ab$. Let $x=2^b-1$, and $y=1+2^b+2^{2b}+\dots+2^{(a-1)b}$. Then 

$$
\begin{aligned}  
xy&=(2^b -1)\cdot (1+2^b+2^{2b}+\dots+2^{(a-1)b})\\
&=2^b\cdot(1+2^b+2^{2b}+\dots+2^{(a-1)b})-(1+2^b+2^{2b}+\dots+2^{(a-1)b})\\
&=(2^b+2^{2b}+\dots+2^{ab})-(1+2^b+2^{2b}+\dots+2^{(a-1)b})\\
&=2^{ab}-1\\
&=2^n-1
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $b<n$, we can conclude that $x=2^b-1<2^n-1$, Also, since $ab=n>a$, it follows that $b>1$. Therefore, $x=2^b-1>2^1-1=1$, so $y<xy=2^n-1$. Thus, we have shown that $2^n-1$ can be written as the product of two positive integers $x$ and $y$, both of which are smaller than $2^n-1$, so $2^n-1$ is not prime.

Theorem 3.7.2. There are infinitely many prime numbers.

Proof. Suppose there are only finitely many prime numbers. Let $p_{1}$, $p_{2}$, $\dots$, $p_{n}$ be a list of all prime numbers. Let $m=p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{n}+1$. Note that $m$ is not divisible by $p_{1}$, since dividing $m$ by $p_{1}$ gives a  quotient of $p_{2}p_{3}\dots p_{n}$ and a remainder of $1$. Similarly, $m$ is not divisible by any of $p_{2}$, $p_{3}$, $\dots$, $p_{n}$.

We now use the fact that every integer larger than $1$ is either prime or can be written as a product of primes. (We'll see a proof of this fact in Chapter 6 — see Theorem 6.4.2.) Clearly $m$ is larger than $1$, so $m$ is either prime or a product of primes. Suppose first that $m$ is prime.  Note that $m$ is larger than all of the numbers in the list $p_{1}$, $p_{2}$, $\dots$, $p_{n}$, so we've found a prime number not in this list. But this contradicts our assumption that this was a list of all prime numbers.

Now suppose $m$ is a product of primes. Let $q$ be one of the primes in this product. Then $m$ is divisible by $q$. But we've already seen that $m$ is not divisible by any of the numbers in the list $p_{1}$, $p_{2}$, $\dots$, $p_{n}$, so once again we have a contradiction with the assumption that this list included all prime numbers.

Since the contradiction that there are finitely many prime numbers has led to a contradiction, there must be infinitely many prime numbers.

Theorem 3.7.3. For every positive integer $n$, there is a sequence of $n$ consecutive positive integers containing no primes.

Proof. Suppose $n$ is a positive integer. Let $x=(n+1)!+2$. We will show that none of the numbers $x$, $x+1$, $x+2$, $\dots$, $x+(n-1)$ is prime. Since this is a sequence of $n$ consecutive positive integers, this will prove the theorem.

To see that $x$ is not prime, note that 
$$
\begin{align}
x&=1\cdot 2 \cdot 3\cdot 4 \dots(n+1) + 2 \\
&=2\cdot(1\cdot 3 \cdot 4\dots(n+1)+ 1)
\end{align}

$$
Thus, $x$ can be written as a product of two smaller positive integers, so $x$ is not prime. 

Similarly, we have
$$
x+1=3\cdot(1\cdot 2\cdot 4\dots(n+1)+1)
$$
so $x+1$ is also not prime. In general, consider any number $x+i$, where $0\leq i\leq n-1$. Then we have
$$
\begin{align}
x+i&=1\cdot2\cdot 3\cdot 4\dots(n+1)+(i+2) \\
&=(i+2)(1\cdot 2\cdot 3\dots(i+1)\cdot(i+3)\dots(n+1)+1)\text{,}
\end{align}


$$
so $x+i$ is not prime.



Theorem. There is a unique real number $m$ with the following two properties:

1.For every real number $x$, $x^2+2x+3\geq m$.
2.If $y$ is any real number with the property that for every real number $x$, $x^2+2x+3\geq y$, then $m\geq y$.

Proof. Existence: Let $m=2$. To prove property 1, let $x$ be an arbitrary real number. Then 
$$
x^2+2x+3=(x+1)^2+2\geq 2=m,
$$
as required. This shows that $2$ is a lower bound for $x^2+2x+3$.

For property 2, let $y$ be an arbitrary number with the property number with the property that for every $x$, $x^2+2x+3\geq y$. In particular, setting $x=-1$ we find that
$$
y\leq (-1)^2+2(-1)+3=2=m\text{.}
$$
Since $y$ was arbitrary, this proves property 2.

Uniqueness: Suppose $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ both have properties 1 and 2. In other words, $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$ are both lower bounds for $x^2+2x+3$, and also if $y$ is any lower bound, then $m_{1}\geq y$ and $m_{2}\geq y$. Applying this last fact to both $y=m_{1}$ and $y=m_{2}$ we get $m_{1} \geq m_{2}$ and $m_{2}\geq m_{1}$, so $m_{1}=m_{2}$.

(a) $\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(A)$.
(b) $\forall B(\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(B)\to A\subseteq B)$.


Proof. Existence: Let $A=\bigcup \mathcal{F}$. To prove property (a), Let $x$ be arbitrary. Suppose $x \in \mathcal{F}$. Let $y$ be arbitrary. Suppose $y\in x$. Then $y\in \bigcup \mathcal{F}$. Thus, $x\subseteq\bigcup \mathcal{F}$. Then $x \in \mathscr{P}\left( \bigcup \mathcal{F} \right)$. Therefore, $\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}\left( \bigcup \mathcal{F} \right)$. Since $A=\bigcup \mathcal{F}$, $\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(A)$.

To prove property (b). Let $B$ be arbitrary. Suppose $\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(B)$. Suppose $x \in \bigcup \mathcal{F}$. Then we can choose some $C\in \mathcal{F}$ such that $x \in C$. Since $\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(B)$, $C\in \mathscr{P}(B)$. Then $C\subseteq B$. Thus, $x \in B$. Therefore, $\bigcup \mathcal{F}\subseteq B$. Since $A=\bigcup \mathcal{F}$, $A\subseteq B$.

Uniqueness: Suppose $A_{1}$ and$A_{2}$ both have properties 1 and 2. In other words, $\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(A_{1})$ and $\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(A_{2})$. $\forall B(\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(B)\to A_{1}\subseteq B)$ and $\forall B(\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathscr{P}(B)\to A_{2}\subseteq B)$. Applying $B=A_{2}$ and $B=A_{1}$ to the last two fact, then we have $A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}$ and $A_{2}\subseteq A_{1}$. Thus, $A_{1}=A_{2}$. Therefore, $A=\bigcup \mathcal{F}$ is unique.
