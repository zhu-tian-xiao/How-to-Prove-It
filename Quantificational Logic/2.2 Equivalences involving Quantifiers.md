# Equivalences involving Quantifiers
Quantifier Negation laws:
$\neg \exists xP(x)$ is equivalent to $\forall x\neg P(x)$.
$\neg \forall xP(x)$ is equivalent to $\exists x\neg P(x)$.

We will write $\exists!xP(x)$ to represent the statement "There is exactly one value of $x$ such that $P(x)$ such that $P(x)$ is true." In general we can think of $\exists!xP(x)$ as an abbreviation for the formula $\exists x(P(x)\land \neg \exists y(P(y)\land y\neq x))$.

$\neg \forall x \in A\,P(x)$ is equivalent to $\neg \forall x(x \in A\to P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\exists x\neg(x \in A\to P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\exists x\neg(x \not\in A\lor P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\exists x(x \in A\land \neg P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\exists x \in A\neg P(x)$.

$\neg \exists x \in A\,P(x)$ is equivalent to $\neg \exists x(x \in A\land P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\forall x\neg(x \in A\land P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\forall x(x\not\in A\lor \neg P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\forall x(x \in A\to \neg P(x))$,
which is equivalent to $\forall x \in A\neg P(x)$.

$\forall x \in A\,P(x)$ is equivalent to $\neg \neg \forall x \in A\,P(x)$,
which is equivalent to $\neg \exists x \in A\,\neg P(x)$.

If $A=\emptyset$ then $\forall x \in A\,P(x)$ will be true, no matter what the statement $P(x)$ is, because, as we have seen, $\exists x \in A\,\neg P(x)$ must be false. Thus, $\forall x \in A\,P(x)$ is always true iff $A=\emptyset$. Mathematicians sometimes say that such a statement is **vacuously** true.

$\forall x(E(x)\land T(x))$ is equivalent to $\forall xE(x)\land \forall xT(x)$. In other words, we could say that **the universal quantifier distributes over conjunction.**^equivalence1

As an application of the distributive law for the universal quantifier and conjunction, suppose $A$ and $B$ are sets and consider the equation $A=B$. We know that two sets are equal when they have exactly the same elements. Thus, the equation $A=B$ means $\forall x(x \in A\leftrightarrow x \in B)$, which is equivalent to $\forall x[(x \in A\to x \in B)\land(x \in B\to x \in A)]$, Because the universal quantifier distributes over conjunction, this is equivalent to the formula $\forall x(x \in A\to x \in B)\land \forall x(x \in B\to x \in A)$, and by the definition fo subset this means $A\subseteq B\land B\subseteq A$. Thus, we have shown that **the equation $A=B$ is also equivalent to the formula $A\subseteq B\land B\subseteq A$.**

We have now introduced seven basic logical symbols: the connective $\land,\lor,\neg,\to,$ and $\leftrightarrow$, and the quantifier $\forall$ and $\exists$. It is a remarkable fact that **the structure of all mathematical statements can be understood using these symbols**, and all mathematical reasoning can be analyzed in terms of the proper use of these symbols. To illustrate the power of the symbols we have introduced, we conclude this section by writing out  a few more mathematical statements in logical notation.
