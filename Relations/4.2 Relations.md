Definition 4.2.1. Suppose $A$ and $B$ are sets. Then a set $R\subseteq A\times B$ is called a relation from $A$ to $B$.

Definition 4.2.2. Here are some examples of relations from one set to another.

1.Let $A=\{ 1,2,3 \}$, $B=\{ 3,4,5 \}$, and $R=\{ (1,3),(1,5),(3,3) \}$. Then $R\subseteq A\times B$, so $R$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$.

2.Let $G=\{ (x,y)\in \mathbb{R}\times \mathbb{R}\mid x>y \}$. Then $G$ is a relation from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$.

3.Let $A=\{ 1,2 \}$ and $B=\mathscr{P}(A)=\{ \emptyset,\{ 1 \},\{ 1,2 \} \}$. Let $E=\{ (x,y)\in A\times B \mid x \in y \}$. Then $E$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$. In this case, $E=\{ (1,\{ 1 \}) \}$

According to the definition, any subset of $A\times B$ is to be called a relation from $A$ to $B$.

Definition 4.2.3. Suppose $R$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$. Then the domain of $R$ is the set 
$$
\text{Dom}(R)=\{ a\in A\mid  \exists b\in B((a,b)\in R)\}\text{.}
$$
The range of $R$ is the set
$$
\text{Ran}(R)=\{ b\in R\mid  \exists a\in A((a,b)\in R) \}\text{.}
$$
The inverse of $R$ is the relation $R^{-1}$ from $B$ to $A$ defined as follows:
$$
R^{-1}=\{ (b,a)\in B\times A\mid (a,b)\in R \}\text{.}
$$
Finally, suppose $R$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$ and $S$ is a relation from $B$ to $C$. Then the composition of $S$ and $R$ is the relation $S\circ R$ from $A$ to $C$ defined as follows:
$$
S\circ R=\{ (a,c)\in A\times C\mid \exists b\in B((a,b)\in R \text{ and }(b,c)\in S )\}.
$$
Note that we have assumed that the second coordinates of pairs in $\mathbb{R}$ and the first coordinates of pairs in $S$ both come from the same set $B$, because that is the situation in which we will most often be interested in $S\circ R$. However, this restriction is not really neccessary, as we ask you to show in exercise 15.
