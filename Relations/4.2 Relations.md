Definition 4.2.3. Suppose $R$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$. Then the domain of $R$ is the set 
$$
\text{Dom}(R)=\{ a\in A\mid  \exists b\in B((a,b)\in R)\}\text{.}
$$
The range of $R$ is the set
$$
\text{Ran}(R)=\{ b\in R\mid  \exists a\in A((a,b)\in R) \}\text{.}
$$
The inverse of $R$ is the relation $R^{-1}$ from $B$ to $A$ defined as follows:
$$
R^{-1}=\{ (b,a)\in B\times A\mid (a,b)\in R \}\text{.}
$$

S\circ R=\{ (a,c)\in A\times C\mid \exists b\in B((a,b)\in R \text{ and }(b,c)\in S )\}.
$$

Theorem 4.2.5 Suppose $R$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$, $S$ is a relation from $B$ to $C$, and $T$ is a relation from $C$ to $D$. Then:

1.$(R^{-1})^{-1}=R$.

2.$\text{Dom}(R^{-1})=\text{Ran}(R)$.

3.$\text{Ran}(R^{-1})=\text{Dom}(R)$.

4.$T\circ(S\circ R)=(T\circ S)\circ R$

5.$(S\circ R)^{-1}=R^{-1}\circ S^{-1}$

Proof. We will prove 1, 2, and half of 4, and leave the rest as exercises.

1.First of all, note that $R^{-1}$ is a relation from $B$ to $A$, so $(R^{-1})^{-1}$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$, just like $R$. To see that $(R^{-1})^{-1}=R$, let $(a,b)$ be an arbitrary ordered pair in $A\times B$. Then 
$$
(a,b)\in(R^{-1})^{-1} \iff (b,a)\in R^{-1}\iff(a,b)\in R
$$
2.First note that Dom$(R^{-1})$ and Ran$(R)$ are both subsets of $B$. Now let $b$ be an arbitrary element of $B$. Then

$$
b\in \text{Dom}(R^{-1}) \iff \exists a\in A((b,a)\in R^{-1})\iff \exists a\in A((a,b)\in R)\iff b\in \text{Ran}(R)
$$

4.Clearly $T\circ(S\circ{}R)$ and $(T\circ{}S)\circ{}R$ are both relations from $A$ to $D$. Let $(a,d)$ be an arbitrary element of $A\times D$.

First, suppose $(a,d)\in T\circ{}(S\circ{}R)$. By the definition of composition, this means that we can choose some $c\in C$ such that $(a,c)\in S\circ{}R$ and $(c,d)\in T$. Since $(a,c)\in S\circ{}R$, we can again use the definition of composition and choose some $b\in B$ such that $(a,b)\in R$ and $(b,c)\in S$. Now since $(b,c)\in S$ and $(c,d)\in T$, we can conclude that $(b,d)\in T\circ{}S$. Similarly, since $(a,b)\in R$ and $(b,d)\in T\circ{}S$, it follows that $(a,d)\in(T\circ{}S)\circ{}R$.

Now suppose $(a,d)\in(T\circ{}S)\circ{}R$. A similar argument, which is left to the reader, shows that $(a,d)\in T\circ{}(S\circ{}R)$. Thus, $T\circ{}(S\circ{}R)=(T\circ{}S)\circ{}R$.
