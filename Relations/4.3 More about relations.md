... One alternative notation that mathemeticians sometimes use with relations motivatied by the fact that in mathemetics we often express a relationship between two objects $x$ and $y$ by putting some symbol between them. 
For example, the notations $x=y$, $x<y$, $x \in y$, $x\subseteq y$ express four important mathemetical relationships between $x$ and $y$. Imitating these notations, if $R$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$, $x \in A$ and $y\in B$, mathematicians sometimes write $xRy$ to mean $(x,y)\in R$.

Definition 4.3.2. Suppose $R$ is a relation on $A$.

1.$R$ is said to be reflexive on $A$ (or just reflexive, if $A$ is clear from context) if $\forall x \in A(xRx)$, or in other words $\forall x \in A((x,x)\in R)$.
2.$R$ is symmetric if $\forall x \in A\forall y\in A(xRy\to yRx)$.
3.$R$ is transitive if $\forall x \in A\forall y\in A\forall z\in A((xRy\land yRz)\to xRz)$.

Theorem 4.3.4. Suppose $R$ is a relation on a set $A$.

1.$R$ is reflexive iff $i_{A}\subseteq R$, where as before $i_{A}$ is the identity relation on $A$.
2.$R$ is symmetric iff $R=R^{-1}$.
3.$R$ is transitive iff $R\circ{}R\subseteq R$.

Proof. We will prove 2 and leave the proofs of 1 and 3 as exercises 7 and 8.

2.($\to$) Suppose $R$ is symmetric. Let $(x,y)$ be an arbitrary element of $R$. Then $xRy$, so since $R$ is symmetric, $yRx$. Thus, $(y,x)\in R$, so by the definition of $R^{-1}$, $(x,y)\in R^{-1}$. Since $(x,y)$ was arbitrary, it follows that $R\subseteq R^{-1}$.

($\leftarrow$) Suppose $R=R^{-1}$, and lret $x$ and $y$ be arbitrary elements of $A$. Suppose $xRy$. Then $(x,y)\in R$, so since $R=R^{-1}$, $(x,y)\in R^{-1}$. By the definition of $R^{-1}$ this means $(y,x)\in R$, so $yRx$. Thus, $\forall x \in A\forall y\in A(xRy\to yRx)$, so $R$ is symmetric.
